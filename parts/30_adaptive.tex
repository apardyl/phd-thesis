\chapter{Adaptive exploration}
\label{chap:adaptive}

\section{Research scope}
\label{sec:adaptive-scope}

The previous chapter established that the challenge of active visual exploration (AVE) can be formulated as a problem of uncertainty reduction. At each step, the agent looks at the region about which the current model is most uncertain, guided by the entropy of its own internal representations. AME demonstrated this approach to be effective, outperforming prior methods that required separately trained exploration modules. However, that work inherited a structural assumption shared by virtually all prior methods in the field: glimpses are sampled from a \emph{fixed-scale, grid-aligned} partition of the scene. Each observation covers a predetermined rectangular region at a predetermined resolution, and the agent's role reduces to choosing cells of a fixed grid.

The rigid grid is not unique to active exploration; it is inherited from the underlying vision transformer, and several lines of work have tried to relax it, each from a different angle. Hierarchical backbones such as Swin~\cite{liu2021Swin} and PVT~\cite{wang2021pyramid} extract features at several fixed resolutions internally, merging tokens as depth increases, but the input tokenisation itself is still a single, uniform grid. FlexiViT~\cite{beyer2023flexivit} and NaViT~\cite{dehghani2024patch} allow a whole image to be tokenised at a different, but still uniform, patch size or aspect ratio, and multi-grid training schedules~\cite{li2021efficient,wu2020multigrid} vary this resolution across training iterations rather than within a single image. None of these approaches let individual patches of the same image differ in scale or sit off-grid, which is the capability an exploration agent would need once some regions of a scene warrant a closer look than others.

A similar grid dependence limits existing active-exploration policies. As described in the previous chapter (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty-scope}) most methods use only fixed size glimpses picked from a grid. However, a notable exception is a family of methods that first captures a single low-resolution overview of the entire scene and then selects a handful of fixed-scale, higher-resolution glimpses on top of it. Several train a reinforcement-learning policy to pick glimpse locations, from the original recurrent attention model~\cite{ba2015multiple} to variants specialised for pretrained hard attention~\cite{elsayed2019saccader}, remote sensing optimisation~\cite{uzkent2020learning}, and early-exiting classification~\cite{wang2020glance}. Others replace the learned policy with a differentiable saliency-based warping layer~\cite{recasens2018learning} or a top-down traversal of image scale-space~\cite{papadopoulos2021hard}. Both families fix the scale of every glimpse in advance and only ever vary its position.

This grid-based assumption does not reflect the physical reality of the platforms that motivate active visual exploration. A simple pan-tilt-zoom (PTZ) camera can freely vary its optical zoom factor, capturing a wide, low-resolution overview of a room or a narrow, high-resolution crop of a single object. An unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) can achieve the same effect by changing altitude, and even a robot with a fixed-aperture camera can emulate variable scale by moving in the environment. What these systems share is a continuous three-dimensional action space over position \emph{and} scale, not a discrete set of fixed-size grid cells.

Beyond the hardware argument, there is an information-theoretic case for variable-scale sampling. When an agent begins exploring an unknown scene, it has no prior over where informative content is located. The rational first action is therefore to observe the scene at the \emph{widest possible field of view}. A single low-resolution glimpse that spans the whole scene establishes a global context -- rough object locations, dominant regions, salient colour or texture blobs. Then, the agent can identify sub-regions that are most likely to improve its current prediction, and concentrate subsequent, higher-resolution observations there. This \emph{coarse-to-fine} strategy minimises the number of observations needed to reach a target accuracy because it avoids wasting high-resolution observations on uninformative background before the agent knows where the most informative regions are. This pattern mirrors how biological visual systems combine rapid saccades with a high-acuity fovea~\cite{yarbus1967eye,curcio1990human}. Fixed sampling grid methods forfeit this strategy entirely. Every glimpse, regardless of when it is taken in the exploration sequence, carries the same spatial extent and the same pixel density.

This chapter addresses this discrepancy between the hardware capabilities and the assumptions embedded in existing methods. Two research questions guide the work:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can a vision transformer backbone be made \emph{elastic} -- robust and accurate when patches are of varying scales and non-grid-aligned positions, rather than rigid $16\times16$ tokens drawn from a fixed grid?
    \item Given such an elastic backbone, can a reinforcement-learning policy discover an effective coarse-to-fine exploration strategy that selects glimpses in the fully continuous three-dimensional space of position and scale to achieve more efficient exploration than grid-constrained methods?
\end{enumerate}

The two publications covered in this chapter answer these questions in sequence. ``Beyond Grids: Exploring Elastic Input Sampling for Vision Transformers''~\cite{pardyl2023grids} tackles the first question. It formalises what \emph{input elasticity} means for a ViT and shows that standard architectures are fragile under the perturbations that arise in real-world embodied scenarios. It then introduces ElasticViT, a modified architecture and training regime that substantially improves robustness, together with two adaptive patch-sampling strategies that demonstrate how to leverage this elasticity in real tasks. ``AdaGlimpse: Active Visual Exploration with Arbitrary Glimpse Position and Scale''~\cite{pardyl2024adaglimpse} then builds on ElasticViT to answer the second question. It formulates glimpse selection over a continuous position-scale action space as a Markov Decision Process and trains a Soft Actor-Critic agent to solve it, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance on reconstruction, classification, and segmentation while needing to observe substantially fewer pixels than previous methods.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:adaptive-preliminaries}

Before describing the two publications, we introduce three background concepts that are not covered in the previous chapter, but are necessary to make this thesis self-contained: positional encodings in vision transformers, reinforcement learning with continuous action spaces and the Soft Actor-Critic algorithm.

\subsection{Positional encodings in vision transformers}
\label{sec:prelim-pos-enc}

The transformer architecture processes its input as an unordered \emph{set} of tokens. The self-attention mechanism is permutation-equivariant and contains no built-in notion of where a token sits in the sequence~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. For language, positional encodings inject order information by adding a position-dependent vector to each token embedding before the first transformer block. For images, they serve the same purpose but must encode spatial location rather than sequence order.

The original ViT~\cite{dosovitskiy2021image} uses \emph{learnable} positional embeddings -- a separate trainable vector is assigned to each cell of the fixed $14\times14$ patch grid (for a $224\times224$ image with $16\times16$ patches), and these vectors are added to the corresponding patch embeddings. The learned embeddings can capture spatial proximity, relative distances, and even some 2D structure, but only for the specific grid size seen during training. Applying a model with $14\times14$ learned positional embeddings to a different grid size requires interpolation of the embeddings, which degrades accuracy.

An alternative used in the original Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention} and adapted to images is the \emph{fixed sinusoidal} encoding. For a one-dimensional sequence position $\mathrm{pos}$ and embedding dimension index $i$, the encoding is:
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{PE}_{(\mathrm{pos},\,2i)} &= \sin\!\left(\mathrm{pos}\,/\,10000^{2i/d}\right), \notag \\
    \mathrm{PE}_{(\mathrm{pos},\,2i+1)} &= \cos\!\left(\mathrm{pos}\,/\,10000^{2i/d}\right),
    \label{eq:sincos-enc}
\end{align}
where $d$ is the embedding dimensionality. For 2D images, row and column positions can be encoded independently and concatenated (or summed). Because sinusoidal encodings are deterministic functions of position rather than learned parameters, they generalise somewhat better to unseen sequence lengths. A model trained on 196 tokens can be applied to more tokens by simply evaluating the same function at new positions, without any interpolation.

Both approaches, however, share a fundamental limitation for the work presented in this chapter. They map from a discrete, finite set of grid positions to embedding vectors. As Section~\ref{sec:beyond} will show, overcoming this limitation, enabling a ViT to process patches at any pixel-level position and at any scale, requires a genuinely continuous positional encoding.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning with continuous actions}
\label{sec:prelim-rl}

Many sequential decision problems can be formalised as a \emph{Markov Decision Process} (MDP)~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement}, a tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma)$ consisting of a state space $\mathcal{S}$, an action space $\mathcal{A}$, a transition distribution $\mathcal{T}(s' \mid s, a)$ specifying the probability of reaching state $s'$ after taking action $a$ in state $s$, a reward function $\mathcal{R}(s, a)$, and a discount factor $\gamma \in [0, 1]$. The goal of a reinforcement learning (RL) agent is to find a \emph{policy} $\pi(a \mid s)$ (a distribution over actions conditioned on the current state) that maximises the expected cumulative discounted reward:
\begin{equation}
    J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t\, r_t\right].
    \label{eq:rl-objective}
\end{equation}
A central quantity used during training is the \emph{action-value function} (Q-function), 
\begin{equation}
    Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t r_t \mid s_0 = s,\, a_0 = a\right],
\end{equation}
which estimates the expected return when starting from state $s$, taking action $a$, and then following policy $\pi$ thereafter. The Q-function satisfies the \emph{Bellman equation}:
\begin{equation}
    Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathcal{R}(s,a) + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{s' \sim \mathcal{T},\, a' \sim \pi}\!\left[Q^\pi(s', a')\right],
    \label{eq:bellman}
\end{equation}
which expresses the value of a state-action pair as the immediate reward plus the discounted value of all future states. This recursive structure is the basis of most practical RL algorithms: a parameterised critic network $Q_\phi(s, a)$ is trained to satisfy Equation~\ref{eq:bellman} by minimising the \emph{Bellman residual}, and the policy is then improved by acting greedily with respect to the current critic.

When the action space $\mathcal{A}$ is continuous (as it is in our case), selecting the greedy action requires maximising $Q_\phi(s, \cdot)$ over a continuous domain, which cannot be done by exhaustive enumeration. The standard solution is the \emph{actor-critic} architecture. A separate actor network $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ is trained to produce actions that maximise $Q_\phi$, while the critic $Q_\phi$ is updated independently via Bellman residual minimisation. Both networks are trained end-to-end with gradient descent.

On-policy algorithms such as REINFORCE~\cite{williams1992simple}, used by early recurrent attention models~\cite{mnih2014recurrent}, update the actor only from trajectories sampled under the \emph{current} policy. This leads to high variance and poor sample efficiency, particularly problematic when the reward is sparse or delayed, as in exploration tasks where performance only improves after several observations have been made. Every time the policy changes even slightly, all previously collected experience becomes invalid.

\subsection{Soft Actor-Critic}
\label{sec:prelim-sac}
The \emph{Soft Actor-Critic} (SAC) algorithm~\cite{haarnoja2018soft} addresses these challenges through two key ideas. First, it is \emph{off-policy} and maintains a \emph{replay buffer} of past transitions $(s, a, r, s')$ collected under any previous version of the policy, updating both actor and critic by sampling mini-batches from this buffer. This decouples data collection from learning, allows each transition to be reused many times, and greatly improves sample efficiency. Second, SAC augments the reward with an entropy bonus, replacing the standard objective (Equation~\ref{eq:rl-objective}) with the \emph{maximum-entropy} objective:
\begin{equation}
    J_{\mathrm{SAC}}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_t \gamma^t \bigl(r_t + \alpha\, H(\pi(\cdot \mid s_t))\bigr)\right],
    \label{eq:sac-objective}
\end{equation}
where $H(\pi(\cdot \mid s_t)) = -\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[\log \pi(a \mid s_t)]$ is the differential entropy of the policy at state $s_t$ and $\alpha > 0$ is a temperature coefficient that balances reward maximisation against entropy maximisation. Maximising entropy encourages the agent to maintain a diverse, spread-out distribution over actions rather than prematurely collapsing to a single mode. The agent is therefore penalised for becoming overly deterministic before it has sufficient evidence that one action is clearly superior, which encourages it to explore the action space more.

In practice, the actor network $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ outputs the parameters (mean and log-variance) of a Gaussian distribution over actions. The continuous action $a \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is sampled from this distribution and passed through a $\tanh$ non-linearity to map it into a bounded action range. Actor and critic are trained alternately: the critic minimises the soft Bellman residual against a bootstrapped target computed with a slowly-updated \emph{target network}, and the actor maximises the expected soft Q-value (i.e. Q-value plus entropy bonus) via the reparameterisation trick, which allows gradients to flow through the sampling operation.
