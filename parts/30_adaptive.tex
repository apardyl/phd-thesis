\chapter{Adaptive exploration}
\label{chap:adaptive}

\section{Research scope}
\label{sec:adaptive-scope}

The previous chapter established that the challenge of active visual exploration can be recast as a problem of uncertainty reduction. At each step, the agent looks at the region about which the current model is most uncertain, guided by the entropy of its own internal representations. AME demonstrated this approach to be effective, outperforming prior methods that required separately trained exploration modules. However, that work inherited a structural assumption shared by virtually all prior methods in the field: glimpses are sampled from a \emph{fixed-scale, grid-aligned} partition of the scene. Each observation covers a predetermined rectangular region at a predetermined resolution, and the agent's role is reduced to playing a grid game.

This assumption does not reflect the physical reality of the platforms that motivate active visual exploration. A simple pan-tilt-zoom camera can freely vary its optical zoom factor, capturing a wide, low-resolution overview of a room or a narrow, high-resolution crop of a single object. An unmanned aerial vehicle can achieve the same effect by changing altitude, and even a robot with a fixed-aperture camera can emulate variable scale by moving in the environment. What these systems share is a continuous two-dimensional action space over position \emph{and} scale, not a discrete set of fixed-size grid cells.

Beyond the hardware argument, there is a purely information-theoretic case for variable-scale sampling. When a scene is first observed, the agent has no knowledge of what it contains or where interesting content lies. The most efficient first observation is therefore the widest possible one: a single low-resolution glimpse that establishes a rough global context while consuming only a fraction of the pixel budget. Once a region of interest is identified, subsequent glimpses can zoom in progressively, concentrating resolution exactly where it is needed. This \emph{coarse-to-fine} strategy is how biological visual systems work. The human eye performs rapid saccades across a scene, with the fovea delivering high-resolution detail only to the small region currently fixated~\cite{sandini2003retina}. Grid-constrained methods forfeit this strategy entirely. Every glimpse, regardless of when it is taken in the exploration sequence, carries the same spatial extent and the same pixel density.

This chapter addresses this discrepancy between the hardware capabilities and the assumptions embedded in existing methods. Two research questions guide the work:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can a vision transformer backbone be made \emph{elastic} -- robust and accurate when patches are of varying scales and non-grid-aligned positions, rather than rigid $16\times16$ tokens drawn from a fixed grid?
    \item Given such an elastic backbone, can a reinforcement-learning policy discover an effective coarse-to-fine exploration strategy that selects glimpses in the fully continuous three-dimensional space of position and scale, and does this yield substantially more efficient exploration than grid-constrained methods?
\end{enumerate}

The two publications covered in this chapter answer these questions in sequence. ``Beyond Grids: Exploring Elastic Input Sampling for Vision Transformers''~\cite{pardyl2023grids} tackles the first question. It formalises what \emph{input elasticity} means for a ViT and shows that standard architectures are fragile under the perturbations that arise in real-world embodied scenarios. Finally, it introduces ElasticViT, a modified architecture and training regime that dramatically improves robustness, together with two adaptive patch-sampling strategies that demonstrate how to levarage this elasticity in real tasks. ``AdaGlimpse: Active Visual Exploration with Arbitrary Glimpse Position and Scale''~\cite{pardyl2024adaglimpse} then builds on ElasticViT to answer the second question. It formulates glimpse selection over a continuous position-scale action space as a Markov Decision Process and trains a Soft Actor-Critic agent to solve it, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance on reconstruction, classification, and segmentation while needing to observe substantially fewer pixels than previous methods.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:adaptive-preliminaries}

Before describing the two publications, we introduce three background concepts that are not covered in the previous chapter, but are necessary to understand the methods presented here: the motivation for coarse-to-fine visual processing, positional encodings in vision transformers, and reinforcement learning with continuous action spaces.

\subsection{Positional encodings in vision transformers}
\label{sec:prelim-pos-enc}

The transformer architecture processes its input as an unordered \emph{set} of tokens: the self-attention mechanism is permutation-equivariant and contains no built-in notion of where a token sits in the sequence. For language, positional encodings inject order information by adding a position-dependent vector to each token embedding before the first transformer block. For images, they serve the same purpose but must encode spatial location rather than sequence order.

The original ViT~\cite{dosovitskiy2021image} uses \emph{learnable} positional embeddings: a separate trainable vector is assigned to each cell of the fixed $14\times14$ patch grid (for a $224\times224$ image with $16\times16$ patches), and these vectors are added to the corresponding patch embeddings. This is the most common approach in practice. The learned embeddings can capture spatial proximity, relative distances, and even some 2D structure, but only for the specific grid size seen during training. Applying a model with $14\times14$ learned positional embeddings to a different grid size requires interpolation of the embeddings, which degrades accuracy.

An alternative used in the original Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention} and adapted to images is the \emph{fixed sinusoidal} encoding. For a one-dimensional sequence position $\mathrm{pos}$ and embedding dimension index $i$, the encoding is:
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{PE}_{(\mathrm{pos},\,2i)} &= \sin\!\left(\mathrm{pos}\,/\,10000^{2i/d}\right), \notag \\
    \mathrm{PE}_{(\mathrm{pos},\,2i+1)} &= \cos\!\left(\mathrm{pos}\,/\,10000^{2i/d}\right),
    \label{eq:sincos-enc}
\end{align}
where $d$ is the embedding dimensionality. For 2D images, row and column positions can be encoded independently and concatenated (or summed). Because sinusoidal encodings are deterministic functions of position rather than learned parameters, they generalise somewhat better to unseen sequence lengths -- a model trained on 196 tokens can be applied to more tokens by simply evaluating the same function at new positions, without any interpolation.

Both approaches, however, share a fundamental limitation for the work presented in this chapter. They map from a discrete, finite set of grid positions to embedding vectors. As Section~\ref{sec:beyond} will show that overcoming this limitation, enabling a ViT to process patches at any pixel-level position and at any scale, requires a genuinely continuous positional encoding.

\subsection{Coarse-to-fine visual processing}
\label{sec:prelim-coarse-to-fine}

When an agent begins exploring an unknown scene, it has no prior over where informative content is located. The rational first action is therefore to observe the scene at the \emph{widest possible field of view}. A single low-resolution glimpse that spans the whole scene costs very little of the observation budget yet establishes a global context -- rough object locations, dominant regions, salient colour or texture blobs. With this context in hand, the agent can identify sub-regions that are most likely to improve its current prediction, and concentrate subsequent, higher-resolution observations there. This \emph{coarse-to-fine} strategy minimises the number of observations needed to reach a target accuracy because it avoids wasting high-resolution observations on uninformative background before the agent knows where the foreground is.

This decision-making logic maps naturally onto the physical capabilities of the platforms that motivate this work. A pan-tilt-zoom (PTZ) camera begins an inspection task at its minimum zoom level, capturing an overview of the monitored area. Once a region of interest is detected (a person entering a restricted zone, an anomalous object on a factory floor), it zooms in to collect the detail needed for precise recognition or measurement. An unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) performing scene understanding over a large area follows an analogous pattern. An initial high-altitude pass provides a low-resolution map of the entire area, and the vehicle then descends to low altitude and visits only the sectors that appear to warrant closer inspection. In both cases, variable scale is not merely a convenience but a prerequisite for efficient operation. A fixed-scale sensor would either miss context (if too narrow) or waste resolution on uninformative regions (if too wide).

\subsection{Reinforcement learning with continuous actions}
\label{sec:prelim-rl}

Many sequential decision problems can be formalised as a \emph{Markov Decision Process} (MDP)~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement}, a tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma)$ consisting of a state space $\mathcal{S}$, an action space $\mathcal{A}$, a transition distribution $\mathcal{T}(s' \mid s, a)$ specifying the probability of reaching state $s'$ after taking action $a$ in state $s$, a reward function $\mathcal{R}(s, a)$, and a discount factor $\gamma \in [0, 1]$. The goal of a reinforcement learning (RL) agent is to find a \emph{policy} $\pi(a \mid s)$ (a distribution over actions conditioned on the current state) that maximises the expected cumulative discounted reward:
\begin{equation}
    J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t\, r_t\right].
    \label{eq:rl-objective}
\end{equation}
A central quantity used during training is the \emph{action-value function} (Q-function), 
\begin{equation}
    Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t r_t \mid s_0 = s,\, a_0 = a\right],
\end{equation}
which estimates the expected return when starting from state $s$, taking action $a$, and then following policy $\pi$ thereafter. The Q-function satisfies the \emph{Bellman equation}:
\begin{equation}
    Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathcal{R}(s,a) + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{s' \sim \mathcal{T},\, a' \sim \pi}\!\left[Q^\pi(s', a')\right],
    \label{eq:bellman}
\end{equation}
which expresses the value of a state-action pair as the immediate reward plus the discounted value of all future states. This recursive structure is the basis of most practical RL algorithms: a parameterised critic network $Q_\phi(s, a)$ is trained to satisfy Equation~\ref{eq:bellman} by minimising the \emph{Bellman residual}, and the policy is then improved by acting greedily with respect to the current critic.

When the action space $\mathcal{A}$ is continuous (as it is in our case), selecting the greedy action requires maximising $Q_\phi(s, \cdot)$ over a continuous domain, which cannot be done by exhaustive enumeration. The standard solution is the \emph{actor-critic} architecture. A separate actor network $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ is trained to produce actions that maximise $Q_\phi$, while the critic $Q_\phi$ is updated independently via Bellman residual minimisation. Both networks are trained end-to-end with gradient descent.

On-policy algorithms such as REINFORCE~\cite{mnih2014recurrent} update the actor only from trajectories sampled under the \emph{current} policy. This leads to high variance and poor sample efficiency, particularly problematic when the reward is sparse or delayed, as in exploration tasks where performance only improves after several observations have been made. Every time the policy changes even slightly, all previously collected experience becomes invalid.

\textbf{Soft Actor-Critic.} The \emph{Soft Actor-Critic} (SAC) algorithm~\cite{haarnoja2018soft} addresses these challenges through two key ideas. First, it is \emph{off-policy} and maintains a \emph{replay buffer} of past transitions $(s, a, r, s')$ collected under any previous version of the policy, updating both actor and critic by sampling mini-batches from this buffer. This decouples data collection from learning, allows each transition to be reused many times, and dramatically improves sample efficiency. Second, SAC augments the reward with an entropy bonus, replacing the standard objective (Equation~\ref{eq:rl-objective}) with the \emph{maximum-entropy} objective:
\begin{equation}
    J_{\mathrm{SAC}}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_t \gamma^t \bigl(r_t + \alpha\, H(\pi(\cdot \mid s_t))\bigr)\right],
    \label{eq:sac-objective}
\end{equation}
where $H(\pi(\cdot \mid s_t)) = -\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[\log \pi(a \mid s_t)]$ is the differential entropy of the policy at state $s_t$ and $\alpha > 0$ is a temperature coefficient that balances reward maximisation against entropy maximisation. Maximising entropy encourages the agent to maintain a diverse, spread-out distribution over actions rather than prematurely collapsing to a single mode. This serves as an intrinsic exploration bonus: the agent is penalised for becoming overly deterministic before it has sufficient evidence that one action is clearly superior. The temperature $\alpha$ can be held fixed or tuned automatically to match a target entropy level.

In practice, the actor network $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ outputs the parameters (mean and log-variance) of a Gaussian distribution over actions. The continuous action $a \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is sampled from this distribution and passed through a $\tanh$ non-linearity to map it into a bounded action range. The critic $Q_\phi(s, a)$ is typically instantiated as two independent networks whose minimum is taken to reduce overestimation bias~\cite{haarnoja2018soft}. Actor and critic are trained alternately -- the critic minimises the soft Bellman residual against a bootstrapped target computed with a slowly-updated \emph{target network}, and the actor maximises the expected soft Q-value (i.e. Q-value plus entropy bonus) via the reparameterisation trick, which allows gradients to flow through the sampling operation.
