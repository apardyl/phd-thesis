\chapter{Open-world exploration}
\label{chap:openworld}

\section{Research scope}
\label{sec:openworld-scope}

Chapter~\ref{chap:adaptive} demonstrated that efficient active visual exploration can be achieved through learned policies operating in a fully continuous observation space. AdaGlimpse, built on the ElasticViT backbone, enables an agent to direct its attention anywhere in a scene at any resolution, and through reinforcement learning it discovers coarse-to-fine strategies that maximise information gain per observed pixel. Yet a structural assumption runs through all three preceding publications: each scene is bounded, accessible from a single vantage point, and the agent's task is to decide where to look within it. The entire environment is, in principle, reachable without physical relocation. The only question is which parts of it to observe, and in what order.

Real-world embodied agents seldom operate under these conditions. A UAV conducting a search-and-rescue mission over a forest, a robot looking for a misplaced item in a large warehouse, or a rover mapping an unfamiliar terrain all share a challenge that is qualitatively different from glimpse selection. The target may not be within the current field of view at all, and acquiring a new observation requires physical movement through three-dimensional space. The agent must form and execute a search strategy in a world that extends well beyond what it can sense from any single position, and each movement consumes time and energy that cannot be recovered.

This chapter takes a step back from the progression of methods and asks a foundational question: do the models available today possess the capabilities required for this kind of open-world, goal-directed visual search? The publication presented in this chapter, \emph{FlySearch: Exploring how vision-language models explore}~\cite{pardyl2025flysearch}, is a benchmark rather than a method. Its contribution is not an algorithm but a realistic evaluation environment, a structured set of challenges that make the question concrete and measurable, and a detailed analysis of where and how current models fall short, and where to direct future research.

The subjects of evaluation are vision-language models (VLMs): large multimodal systems such as GPT-4o, Claude, and Gemini that process interleaved sequences of images and text to produce language outputs. Treating a VLM as an exploration agent requires no task-specific training. The model is prompted with a natural language description of the target object, receives camera images from a simulated UAV, and outputs navigation commands in text form. This zero-shot setup is attractive for open-world exploration because such environments are too varied for narrow specialist systems, while VLMs encode broad world knowledge that a system trained only in a fixed simulator would lack (Section~\ref{sec:prelim-vlm} discusses this in more detail).

FlySearch's findings show that even the most capable proprietary models substantially underperform humans on basic search tasks, and the performance gap grows as the tasks demand longer, more consistent exploration strategies. Fine-tuning on synthetic trajectories can correct surface-level failures such as instruction following and spatial grounding, but leaves the deeper inability to maintain a coherent, multi-step search plan unaddressed. FlySearch does not resolve open-world exploration so much as define it more precisely: by characterising where current models fail and under what conditions the gap to human performance is largest, it establishes a concrete set of open problems and a reproducible benchmark against which future progress can be measured.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:openworld-preliminaries}

Before describing the publication, for completeness, we introduce three background concepts not addressed in previous chapters: vision-language models, the object-goal navigation task that FlySearch is based on, and the sensing properties of UAVs that shape the search problem.

\subsection{Vision-language models}
\label{sec:prelim-vlm}

Vision-language models (VLMs) are neural networks trained to reason jointly over visual and textual inputs. The dominant architecture couples a large language model with a vision encoder. A high-capacity image encoder, typically a vision transformer (ViT)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021image}, maps an input image to a sequence of visual tokens. These tokens are projected into the language model's embedding space and concatenated or interleaved with textual tokens, forming a unified input to an autoregressive language model backbone. Training on large corpora of image-text pairs, along with instruction-following data and reinforcement learning from human feedback, teaches the model to produce coherent, contextually appropriate text outputs conditioned on both modalities~\cite{achiam2023gpt,team2023gemini}.

Modern VLMs support multi-turn conversations that contain multiple images. The model maintains a record of all past observations in its context window and generates responses that draw on the full history of what it has seen and said. In our case, the model is shown a new camera image at each exploration step, and the context accumulates across steps, so the model can in principle reason about its trajectory, which directions it has already explored, what it has seen so far, and where the target is most likely to be.

What makes VLMs attractive for exploration tasks is zero-shot generalisability. Because they are trained on broad distributions of internet-scale data, they encode extensive world knowledge: where particular objects tend to be found in urban versus rural environments, what a rational systematic search looks like, and how visual cues from a high altitude relate to ground-level objects. Whether this latent knowledge translates into effective sequential decision-making when the model is placed in the control loop of an embodied exploration task is precisely the question FlySearch is designed to answer.

\subsection{Object-goal navigation}
\label{sec:prelim-objectnav}

Object-goal navigation (ObjectNav)~\cite{batra2020objectnav} is a task in which an embodied agent, starting from a random initial position in a simulated environment, must navigate to an instance of a specified object. The agent receives egocentric visual observations and a goal specification, originally a category label and more recently a natural-language description, and must issue movement commands until it is sufficiently close to the target, at which point it signals success with a STOP or FOUND action. The task is different from passive visual search because the agent must actively move through the environment to acquire new observations, making decisions about where to go next based on incomplete, egocentric information.

The dominant simulators for ObjectNav (Habitat-Sim~\cite{savva2019habitat}, AI2-THOR~\cite{kolve2017ai2}, and Gibson~\cite{xia2018gibson}) are almost exclusively indoor. These environments feature relatively small, structured layouts bounded by rooms and corridors, where the total navigable area is limited and explicit floor plans can be constructed and used to guide search. Language-conditioned ObjectNav~\cite{majumdar2022zson,gadre2023cows} extends the standard formulation by replacing discrete category labels with free-form natural language descriptions, requiring the agent to reason about object identity without a pre-defined vocabulary of targets. This is necessary for any deployment in which the set of possible objects is not known at design time.

FlySearch adopts the language-conditioned formulation but departs from prior work in several important respects. The environment is large and outdoor, with no exploitable floor plan or room structure. The agent is embodied in a UAV rather than a ground-based robot, giving it a top-down perspective and a three-dimensional action space. And the benchmark is designed to evaluate VLMs as zero-shot exploration agents rather than purpose-built navigation systems, measuring whether broad pre-training is sufficient for open-world search without any task-specific adaptation.

\subsection{UAV-based visual search}
\label{sec:prelim-uav}

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) offer a distinctive sensing modality. By controlling altitude, the UAV agent controls the trade-off between field of view and visual resolution: at high altitude, the camera captures a large ground area in a single image but at low resolution, making it possible to survey a wide area quickly at the cost of fine detail; at low altitude, the field of view narrows but small or visually ambiguous targets become identifiable. An effective search strategy must manage this trade-off dynamically, flying high to establish a broad situational picture and descending to confirm candidate locations.

This dynamic has a structural parallel to the coarse-to-fine exploration studied in Chapter~\ref{chap:adaptive}, where the agent controlled the scale of its glimpses to alternate between wide-context overviews and high-resolution inspections of specific regions. In the UAV setting, however, scale variation is achieved through physical movement rather than optical zoom: the agent must fly to a different altitude to change its effective resolution. This introduces additional complexity. Changing altitude takes steps that could otherwise be spent moving horizontally, and descending to obtain detail over one region means sacrificing coverage of other regions. A rational search strategy must therefore reason about when the evidence gathered at high altitude is sufficient to justify the cost of a closer inspection, and when it is more valuable to continue surveying from above.

The 3D action space, horizontal movement and altitude together, means that the search problem is higher-dimensional than ground-based navigation. The challenge is as much conceptual as computational: the agent must form and maintain a mental model of what has been explored at which altitude and what regions remain uncertain. Whether VLMs, whose architectures and training objectives were not designed with this kind of spatial bookkeeping in mind, can perform these computations effectively is a central empirical question addressed by FlySearch.
