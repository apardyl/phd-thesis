\chapter{Synthesis and future directions}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This dissertation argues that active visual exploration should be treated as a learning problem in its own right, and the four publications at its core each removed an obstacle on the way from passive perception towards efficient embodied vision. ``Active Visual Exploration Based on Attention-Map Entropy'' (IJCAI 2023)~\cite{pardyl2023active} showed that the uncertainty needed to guide exploration is already present inside a task model, in the entropy of its attention maps, so that no separately trained decision module is required. ``Beyond Grids: Exploring Elastic Input Sampling for Vision Transformers'' (WACV 2025)~\cite{pardyl2023grids} freed the underlying architecture from the rigid patch grid, formalising input elasticity and showing that a suitably trained ViT can process patches of arbitrary position and scale. ``AdaGlimpse: Active Visual Exploration with Arbitrary Glimpse Position and Scale'' (ECCV 2024)~\cite{pardyl2024adaglimpse} built a reinforcement learning agent on top of that backbone and demonstrated that a learned policy over a continuous position-scale action space discovers coarse-to-fine exploration on its own, reaching state-of-the-art performance while observing a fraction of the pixels required by prior methods. Finally, ``FlySearch: Exploring how vision-language models explore'' (NeurIPS 2025)~\cite{pardyl2025flysearch} carried the question to the global scale of open outdoor environments and found that current vision-language models, despite their broad world knowledge, cannot yet form and execute the systematic search strategies that humans apply with ease.

Taken together, these results support the central claim of the thesis at the local scale: exploration can be learned, it can be efficient, and it does not require sensing abstractions that diverge from current computer vision hardware. At the global scale, they chart the frontier instead of closing it. The gap identified by FlySearch, between short-horizon reactive behaviour and consistent multi-step exploration, together with the sim-to-real question raised in Chapter~\ref{chap:openworld}, defines the immediate research agenda that follows from this work.

Parts of this research were carried out within the National Science Centre Preludium grant \emph{Where to look next -- guiding active visual exploration with internal model uncertainty} (2024--2025), of which I was the principal investigator. My work was recognised with the START scholarship of the Foundation for Polish Science (2026).

My ongoing work continues along both directions opened by this thesis. The first project, a direct follow-up to FlySearch, develops a reinforcement learning framework for training vision-language agents to explore real-world environments: models in the 4--11B parameter range are fine-tuned with the GRPO algorithm on procedurally generated navigation tasks and on Gaussian-splat scenes reconstructed from real flight data, and will be evaluated both on the original benchmark and on a physical UAV, addressing the exploration gap and the sim-to-real transfer question at once. The second project pursues self-supervised discovery of discrete, spatially grounded visual concepts, aiming at an interpretable backbone for downstream embodied tasks; it is conducted in collaboration with the Computer Vision group of the Max Planck Institute for Informatics, where I completed a research internship (June--September 2026) under the supervision of Prof. Bernt Schiele.

Beyond the main line of research, I took part in research on AI-assisted medical diagnostics, including sepsis diagnosis from Gram-stained smears, which led to a European patent application, and lung cancer diagnosis from CT scans, as well as work on partial multi-label learning and on benchmarking the spatial reasoning of vision foundation models. During my doctoral studies, I also contributed to national and European research projects (including NCN Sonata Bis and the Horizon Europe ELIAS project), reviewed for NeurIPS, IJCV, WACV, and KDD, and co-organised several machine learning summer schools and the ELLIS Doctoral Symposium. These activities are described in Chapter~\ref{chapter:other}.

Learning to explore, at the scale of a camera as much as at the scale of a city, remains far from solved. This thesis showed that the local part of the problem yields to careful learning-based design; making the same true in the open world is the work that comes next.
