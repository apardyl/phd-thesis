\chapter{Synthesis and future directions}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This dissertation investigated four hypotheses concerning active visual exploration, each through one of its core publications. The results of ``Active Visual Exploration Based on Attention-Map Entropy'' (IJCAI 2023)~\pubcite{pardyl2023active} confirm Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:ame}: the uncertainty encoded in a task model's attention maps can guide exploration without a separately trained decision module. ``Beyond Grids: Exploring Elastic Input Sampling for Vision Transformers'' (WACV 2025)~\pubcite{pardyl2023grids} confirms Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:beyond}: a suitably trained ViT can remain resilient to patches of varying position, scale, and coverage. ``AdaGlimpse: Active Visual Exploration with Arbitrary Glimpse Position and Scale'' (ECCV 2024)~\pubcite{pardyl2024adaglimpse} confirms Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:adaglimpse}: a learned policy over a continuous position-scale action space can discover coarse-to-fine exploration and achieve strong performance while observing fewer pixels. Finally, ``FlySearch: Exploring how vision-language models explore'' (NeurIPS 2025)~\pubcite{pardyl2025flysearch} confirms Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:flysearch}: current vision-language models can handle short-horizon navigation but cannot yet reliably form and execute systematic multi-step search strategies in large open environments.

These confirmations are limited to the models, datasets, tasks, and environments evaluated in the respective publications. Taken together, they trace a progression from using internal model signals to choose where to look, through removing rigid sampling constraints and learning continuous observation policies, to measuring where current models fail when choosing where to move. At the global scale, the gap identified by FlySearch between short-horizon reactive behaviour and consistent multi-step exploration, together with the sim-to-real question raised in Chapter~\ref{chap:openworld}, defines the immediate research agenda that follows from this work.

Parts of this research were carried out within the National Science Centre Preludium grant \emph{Where to look next -- guiding active visual exploration with internal model uncertainty} (2024--2025), of which I was the principal investigator. My work was recognised with the START scholarship of the Foundation for Polish Science (2026).

My ongoing work continues along both directions opened by this thesis. The first project, a direct follow-up to FlySearch, develops a reinforcement learning framework for training vision-language agents to explore real-world environments: models in the 4--11B parameter range are fine-tuned with the GRPO algorithm on procedurally generated navigation tasks and on Gaussian-splat scenes reconstructed from real flight data, and will be evaluated both on the original benchmark and on a physical UAV, addressing the exploration gap and the sim-to-real transfer question at once. The second project pursues self-supervised discovery of discrete, spatially grounded visual concepts, aiming at an interpretable backbone for downstream embodied tasks; it is conducted in collaboration with the Computer Vision group of the Max Planck Institute for Informatics, where I completed a research internship (June--September 2026) under the supervision of Prof. Bernt Schiele.

Beyond the main line of research, I took part in research on AI-assisted medical diagnostics, including sepsis diagnosis from Gram-stained smears, which led to a European patent application, and lung cancer diagnosis from CT scans, as well as work on partial multi-label learning and on benchmarking the spatial reasoning of vision foundation models. During my doctoral studies, I also contributed to national and European research projects (including NCN Sonata Bis and the Horizon Europe ELIAS project), reviewed for NeurIPS, IJCV, WACV, and KDD, and co-organised several machine learning summer schools and the ELLIS Doctoral Symposium. These activities are described in Chapter~\ref{chapter:other}.

Learning to explore, at both the scale of a camera and the scale of a city, remains far from solved. The four hypotheses confirmed in this thesis identify concrete capabilities that are possible in the evaluated local settings and concrete limitations that remain in the open world.
